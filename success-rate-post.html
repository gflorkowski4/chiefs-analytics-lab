Chiefs Analytics Lab · Stat No. 2

Success rate: did the play do its job?

Kansas City Chiefs · 2024–2026 seasons Data: nflfastR play-by-play

Week 9 against the Jets, the Chiefs' offense succeeded on 56.2% of its plays. Not scored on. Not gained big yards on. Succeeded on — 36 of 64 plays did the job they were asked to do, series after series, and the drives kept moving. That is a big reason the EPA from that game looked so good: before a play can add a lot of points, it usually has to keep the drive alive first. Success rate is the stat that counts how often that happens.

In one line

Success rate = the share of plays that gained enough — and “enough” depends on the down.

01

The 40 / 60 / 100 rule

One rule, three thresholds. If a play clears its threshold, it counts as a success — yes or no, nothing in between.

EPA asks how much a play helped. Success rate asks a blunter question: did the play gain enough of the yards it needed? “Enough” is a share of the distance to the first-down marker, and the share gets steeper as the downs run out:

1st down

40%

Gain at least 40% of the yards to go. On 1st & 10, that is 4 yards — stay ahead of the chains and the series is in good shape.

2nd down

60%

Gain at least 60% of the yards to go. On 2nd & 10, that is 6 yards — close enough that one more play can finish the job.

3rd & 4th down

100%

Get all of it. Anything short leaves the first down unmade, so anything short is a failure — even by a yard.

That is the whole stat. Grade every play yes or no by that rule, count the yeses, and divide by the number of plays. Week 9: 36 yeses out of 64 plays is 56.2%. No model, no projection — just a grade on every snap, added up.

02

Two plays, two verdicts

Where EPA and success rate agree — and where they don't. That disagreement is the point of having both.

Play 1 · Where they agree · Week 9 vs. the Jets

Mahomes → Walker, 25 yd TD

A 25-yard touchdown clears every bar there is. It gains far more than the distance it needed, so success rate grades it a plain success — and EPA loves it too, at +3.77, the best play of the Chiefs' Week 9. When a play is this good, the two stats tell the same story in two different languages: EPA says how much it was worth, success rate just says yes.

Play 2 · Where they disagree · The 3rd-down trap

3rd & 10, a gain of 8 yards

Now grade the hardest case in the rule. It is 3rd & 10, and the play gains 8 yards. On the broadcast that looks fine — it is a solid gain, it moves the offense down the field, and a yards column would call it a good play. EPA is at least somewhat forgiving here too: the yards and the field position count for something in its math.

Success rate is not forgiving at all. On 3rd down the rule is 100% — the play needed 10 yards and got 8, so it is graded not a success, full stop. The drive is left facing 4th & 2 instead of a fresh set of downs, and the stat refuses to hand out partial credit for getting close.

Neither stat is wrong. They are cousins, not twins: EPA measures value on a sliding scale, success rate measures reliability with a hard line. An offense full of almost-conversions can post respectable yardage and still stall — success rate is the stat that catches it.

EPA tells you how big the play was. Success rate tells you whether the drive survived it.

03

Success, down by down

2024 regular season — and a warning label on the last row.

Split the 2024 regular season by down and a pattern shows up: the Chiefs succeeded more often as the down got later. That is partly the offense converting when it had to — and partly selection. On 3rd down, every play is called to get the whole distance. On 4th down, teams mostly only go for it when the distance is short and the situation suits them, which stacks the deck before the ball is even snapped.

Read that 70.6% with care: it rests on only 17 fourth-down plays all season. A sample that small swings wildly — a couple of plays the other way and the number drops fast. It says the Chiefs chose their 4th-down spots well in 2024; it does not say they would succeed seven times out of ten if they went for it all the time.

For the season as a whole, the Chiefs finished 2024 at 47.7% on 1,067 plays, dipped to 44.4% in 2025 on 1,045 plays, and sit at 49.0% in 2026 through Week 9 — 237 successes in 484 plays. Across all three seasons in the explorer, the blended rate is 46.5% on 2,752 plays: a little under half of everything the offense runs clears the bar.

04

What success rate misses

A yes-or-no grade is the stat's strength — and its blind spot.

Success rate does not care how big the play was. A 4-yard gain on 1st & 10 and a 40-yard gain on 1st & 10 both clear the 40% bar, so both count exactly the same: one success. The checkdown that barely keeps a drive alive and the bomb that changes the game are worth the same single point in this stat — EPA is the one that tells them apart.

It also flattens context. A play that barely converts and a play that scores both earn a yes, wherever they happen on the field. That is why the two stats belong together: success rate for how often the offense did its job, EPA for how much it mattered when it did.

05

Coming to the explorer

The next stat in the series gets its own column.

Success rate is coming to the Lab's play explorer as its own column, next to EPA — one mark per play showing whether that snap cleared the 40 / 60 / 100 bar. Filter to Week 9 and you will be able to count the same story this post opened with: 36 successes in 64 plays, each one traceable to the snap that earned it.

Now you know how to read it. When the column lands, you will not just see a percentage — you will know exactly what a play had to do to earn its yes, and why an 8-yard gain can still be a no.
